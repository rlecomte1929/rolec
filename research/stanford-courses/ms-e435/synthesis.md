Apoorv Agrawal opens the first session by framing the central question of the course: where is the money in AI, and who will capture it as this ecosystem matures? He positions MS&E 435 as an attempt to answer that question seriously, not through hype or headlines, but through the lens of unit economics, gross margin, and structural analysis.

He begins with a macro framing. Technology has risen from roughly 5% to 13% of global GDP over the past decade. AI, he argues, may represent the next step-change — the biggest technology supercycle since the PC, the Internet, and Mobile. But unlike those prior cycles, AI's value distribution across the stack is radically different.

**The Inverted Triangle**

Agrawal introduces his signature analytical framework: the "inverted triangle." In prior technology cycles — the internet, mobile, cloud — value migrated upward over time. Infrastructure was commoditized; applications captured the margin. Think AWS versus Salesforce: AWS provides the plumbing, but Salesforce earns software-like margins of 70-80%. The value pyramid in those eras was right-side up, with applications at the top capturing the most value.

AI has inverted this. Today, semiconductors and infrastructure dominate revenue and profitability while applications remain comparatively small and low-margin. He provides specific data: NVIDIA alone captured approximately 75% of the $350 billion in new AI revenue added over two years, maintaining 75% gross margins through near-monopolistic GPU control. Application companies — those building products on top of AI — achieve gross margins of between 0% and 30%, far below historical software benchmarks.

The key reason: AI is not software with zero marginal cost. "Every incremental user burns GPUs," he explains. Every token generated requires real compute resources tied to model size, context length, and memory bandwidth. The old 80-90% gross margin software model does not automatically apply when distribution has real, non-trivial marginal cost.

**The Monetization Gap**

He then walks through the monetization data. ChatGPT reaches 900 million to 1 billion weekly active users — a staggering distribution footprint. But it monetizes at roughly $10-$15 per user annually. Compare that to Google Search, which monetizes at $90-$100 per user per year, or social media platforms at $49 per user per year. The gap is enormous.

This reflects a structural issue: AI requires active user engagement rather than passive consumption. A search ad or social feed can be monetized passively. A generative AI response requires compute per query. Advertising may eventually close this gap — ChatGPT's ad format potential is real — but the path is uncertain and the timing is unclear.

**Will the Triangle Ever Flip?**

Agrawal is careful not to say the inverted triangle is permanent. He argues, following the logic of historical tech cycles, that "history rhymes." Applications eventually capture greater value. He points to evidence that the model layer is expanding rapidly: Anthropic is rumored to be at a $45 billion annualized revenue run rate, OpenAI at $30 billion. This suggests the application and model layer is growing fast.

He also highlights that Chinese open-source models — DeepSeek and others — represent an "expansionary" force rather than simply deflationary. The emerging pattern: use frontier models for complex planning tasks while deploying open-source models for execution. This complementarity, rather than replacement, may help application economics.

**The Stack as a Course Structure**

Agrawal uses the remainder of the session to preview the course structure, explaining that each week will examine one layer of the AI stack. Energy and infrastructure come first, because they are currently the most capital-intensive and least understood. Then inference economics, model labs, enterprise software, agents, and finally applications in specific verticals.

He closes with a challenge to students: don't take the inverted triangle as permanent. The question isn't whether the triangle flips — it probably will — but when, at what rate, and which companies will be positioned to benefit from the shift.

Brad Gerstner opens by contextualizing the AI moment in macroeconomic terms. Technology has grown from roughly 5% to 13% of global GDP. AI, he argues, may accelerate productivity at a scale he characterizes as "10 times the Industrial Revolution at 10 times the speed." This is not hyperbole for effect — he is making a GDP-level argument about where the technology wave is positioned.

**Inference as the Economic Bottleneck**

Gerstner frames the session around inference, not training. Training gets most of the public attention — GPT-4's training cost, Gemini's training runs — but inference is where the money flows at scale. Every time a user sends a message to ChatGPT, every time a company runs an agentic workflow, every time synthetic data is generated for fine-tuning — these are inference operations. He quotes NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang: "Everything changed with inference-time reasoning." Huang characterized the shift as inference demand heading not toward 10x or 100x, but "1 billion times" greater than present.

**The Token Economy**

Tokens are the atomic unit of AI value. Global consumption of tokens is already running at tens of trillions per week and climbing. Gerstner walks through the cost structure of token generation: the compute intensity of generating one token scales roughly as parameter size multiplied by context length squared. As models scale toward trillions of parameters and context windows expand to hundreds of thousands of tokens, computational demands escalate exponentially. This is why inference economics, not training economics, will determine which companies survive.

**Inference Cost Deflation: 99% in 2.5 Years**

Sunny Madra presents the data on inference cost trajectories. He states that "inference costs have fallen by about 90% over the past year and about 99% over the past two and a half years." Three forces drive this: (1) semiconductor supply chain advancement and improved fab yields; (2) engineering innovation at the software and systems layer — better batching, better quantization, better kernel optimization; (3) electricity efficiency improvements from new cooling architectures and denser compute configurations.

This cost deflation is transformative for application economics. When inference is 99% cheaper, the gross margin math for application companies fundamentally changes. What cost a dollar now costs a penny. This is the mechanism by which the "inverted triangle" Agrawal described in Week 1 may eventually flip.

**OpenAI and Anthropic's Business Model Bet**

Gerstner explains that OpenAI and Anthropic initially operated at significant losses, betting on two simultaneous curves: (1) inference costs would fall, and (2) AI capability (intelligence per token) would rise. Both happened faster than expected. As evidence of the payoff: Anthropic added "$10 billion in annualized revenue in just the month of March alone" — demonstrating exponential product-market fit inflection.

**NVIDIA's Competitive Position**

Gerstner, despite running a firm that profits from NVIDIA's position as an investment, makes an analytical case that NVIDIA at $4.5 trillion valuation was "undervalued" at 13x P/E ratio. The company had "$1 trillion in bookings for the next eight quarters" — a backlog that suggests demand visibility most companies can only dream of. Despite competition from Amazon (Trainium), Google (TPU), Cerebras, and others, NVIDIA's software ecosystem (CUDA) and systems integration maintain moat.

**Groq's Architecture: Deterministic Dataflow**

Sunny Madra explains Groq's chip architecture in depth. Traditional GPUs use dynamic memory access — data moves between large external DRAM and the compute cores in an unpredictable pattern. Groq's approach is fundamentally different: a "dataflow architecture" where a fully deterministic compiler pre-calculates exactly where every computation will happen and when. This eliminates the memory bandwidth bottleneck that plagues GPU-based inference.

Groq's chips use on-chip SRAM (Static RAM) rather than external DRAM. SRAM is much faster — orders of magnitude — but is also much smaller and more expensive per bit. For inference workloads, where data access patterns are highly regular and predictable, SRAM-centric architecture delivers dramatically higher throughput per watt.

Madra explains the specific innovation: separating "prefill" (processing the input prompt, which is highly parallel and compute-bound) from "decode" (generating each output token, which is sequential and memory-bandwidth-bound). GPUs excel at prefill. Groq's architecture excels at decode. The Groq-NVIDIA NVLink Fusion integration — announced as part of NVIDIA's $20 billion acquisition of Groq — connects the two architectures so each handles the workload it is architecturally optimized for.

The result: "2.5 times the token generation volume within the same power envelope." In a world where data centers are limited by power capacity, tokens-per-watt is the core competitive metric.

**Groq's Go-to-Market Journey**

Madra describes Groq's evolution from hardware vendor to cloud API provider. The pivot was data-driven: direct hardware sales cycles were long and capital-intensive. When Groq launched its cloud API (GroqCloud), it reached 4 million users within weeks — a dramatically faster adoption trajectory than any hardware sales motion had achieved.

**Career Implications: EQ Over IQ**

Gerstner closes with a perspective on what the AI supercycle means for individuals. As AI automates analytical problem-solving — the core of most knowledge work — the skills that AI cannot replicate gain premium value. He specifically names emotional intelligence, relationship-building, persuasion, and strategic leadership. His framing: "IQ will become a commodity, and EQ — the ability to build human relationships — will become more valuable." He advocates for individuals to become "bionic" by deeply leveraging AI tools for sustainable career relevance, while continuing to invest in the fundamentally human skills.

As AI infrastructure optimizes for tokens-per-watt, ReloPass should optimize its own unit economics for requirements-per-corridor-per-hour-of-analyst-time. Lochmiller identifies the primary constraint on AI scaling: not GPUs, not software, not capital — but energized, ready-to-use data center shells. The bottleneck in the AI infrastructure supply chain is the intersection of (1) available power capacity (a utility or grid connection) and (2) a physical structure capable of housing and cooling high-density compute. Next-generation designs are targeting 600 kilowatts per rack and, in some experimental configurations, approaching 1 megawatt per rack. At $60 million per megawatt, a 1-gigawatt facility requires $60 billion in capital.

- A 1 gigawatt privately owned electrical substation

**The AGI Has Arrived Argument**

Ghodsi's AGI argument is not about capabilities in isolation. It is about the gap between what AI can do and what organizations are currently extracting from it. He defines AGI functionally: a system that can perform the cognitive tasks required for most knowledge work at or above human level. By that definition, he argues, the models already exist. GPT-4-class systems and their successors can draft legal briefs, write code, analyze financial statements, synthesize scientific literature. The capability barrier is not the binding constraint.

**The Context Problem is the Real Barrier**

The binding constraint is context. "AI doesn't have an intelligence problem," Ghodsi declares. "It has a context problem." Enterprise agents fail not because they are insufficiently intelligent, but because they lack access to the organizational information they need to operate. A CFO cannot get an AI system to explain why margins changed because the system doesn't know which database holds the authoritative revenue figures, how the company defines "margin" in its particular accounting convention, or when the fiscal quarter closes.

This is an architectural problem, not an organizational one. Enterprise knowledge is scattered across dozens of disconnected platforms — ERP systems, CRM tools, internal wikis, email threads, Slack messages, spreadsheets on individual laptops. The models are ready; the data pipelines are not.

**Data Strategy as the Enterprise Moat**

Ghodsi reframes the competitive question for enterprise AI: the moat is not the model. Models are increasingly commoditized — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and open-source alternatives all provide capable inference. The moat is organizational context: the proprietary data, business process definitions, and domain-specific knowledge that only the enterprise possesses.

He argues that companies which invest in data strategy now — building unified data platforms, creating authoritative data catalogs, establishing clean lineage from raw data to business metrics — will be the ones that can actually deploy agents effectively. Companies that neglect data infrastructure will find that every AI initiative hits the same wall: the model is capable, but the context it needs doesn't exist in an accessible form.

### Top 10 Actionable Insights

1. **AI has real marginal cost; price and architect accordingly.** Unlike traditional software, every AI inference operation consumes compute proportional to model size and context window. Any product built on AI must account for this in its gross margin model, pricing strategy, and system architecture. Zero-marginal-cost software economics do not apply — but costs are falling fast (99% in 2.5 years), so the right response is not to avoid AI but to design for the current cost curve and architect for the future one.

2. **The context problem is almost always the binding constraint.** When AI fails in enterprise settings, it is usually not because the model is insufficiently capable — it is because the model lacks access to the organizational context it needs. Build the context layer (data catalogs, business ontologies, authoritative source definitions) before the AI layer. AI without context is a capable employee on their first day: intelligent but directionless.

3. **Define evals before building anything.** Before introducing any AI component, define precisely what "correct" means for the specific task. Evaluations should be specific, measurable, and grounded in real-world failure modes. The evaluation framework is more valuable than the model choice; it is the tool that enables continuous improvement and prevents regression.

4. **Decouple models from agent logic.** Build AI-powered systems with a provider-agnostic model abstraction layer. The model market is evolving rapidly; hardcoding a specific provider creates lock-in and prevents optimization as better, cheaper models emerge. Treat model providers like cloud providers: standardize on an interface, not an implementation.

5. **Vertical integration of manufacturing beats supply chain dependence.** Crusoe's reduction of power distribution center lead times from 100 weeks to 20 weeks through in-house manufacturing is a general principle: when the supply chain cannot keep pace with your growth, internalize the bottleneck. Applied to software products: when third-party data sources are unreliable or slow to update, build proprietary monitoring and validation.

6. **Inference demand scales exponentially with agentic workloads.** The shift from simple chat (1,000 tokens per interaction) to agentic tasks (100,000+ tokens per interaction) creates a 100x demand multiplier per unit of user value delivered. Any system that expects to serve agentic workflows must architect for this multiplier from the beginning, not retrofit it.

7. **The data wall is navigable via verifiable rewards.** The claim that pre-training data is exhausted overstates the problem. RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards) creates training signal from correct answers, not human preference labels — and correct answers are abundant in any domain with ground truth (code execution, mathematical proof, regulatory compliance verification). Identify your domain's verifiable rewards and build training pipelines around them.

8. **Specialization beats generalization for enterprise economics.** General frontier models are "smart geniuses that know nothing about your business." Enterprise advantage comes from specialized post-training on domain-specific data, evals, and reward functions — not from API access to the largest model. The economic model: invest in evals and data now, migrate high-volume workloads to custom models as they mature, achieve 40-70% gross margins instead of 0-30%.

9. **Open source is validation, not monetization.** The test of a market-worthy product is whether people use it when it's free. Open sourcing a component creates adoption signal and stress-tests product-market fit before investing in monetization infrastructure. The platform flywheel — open source creates adoption, platform captures value from scale — is the proven model for developer-facing products.

10. **Say no strategically to protect product integrity.** In a market where AI capabilities expand continuously, the discipline to reject features and use cases that don't fit the core value proposition is a competitive advantage. Every addition that compromises reliability, speed, or simplicity of the core use case is a strategic error, regardless of how technically interesting it is. The best AI products in the course — Chai's antibody design, Baseten's inference infrastructure, Groq's deterministic architecture — are defined as much by what they exclude as by what they include.

#### 1. Core Architecture Validation and Enhancement

**The Deterministic Rule Engine as the Defensible Asset (Weeks 1, 2, 8)**

The course's consistent finding that value accretes to the infrastructure layer — the deterministic, reliable, verifiable layer — rather than to the application layer validates ReloPass's core architectural choice. This ensures that LLM provider changes, model updates, or AI capability improvements can be incorporated without risking the reliability of the compliance-critical runtime output.

**Directed Acyclic Graph Implementation for Requirement Sequencing (Week 5)**

Katti's description of agentic workloads as directed acyclic graphs is the most important technical insight for improving ReloPass's rule engine. Immigration requirements are not a simple ordered list — they are a DAG with precedence constraints (visa application requires completed health check, which requires GP registration, which requires D-number). The current "lead time chain feasibility" problem (not just per-requirement lead times, but full chain feasibility) is the DAG scheduling problem.

Implementing a proper DAG representation enables:
- Critical path computation (which sequence of requirements determines the earliest possible start date?)
- Bottleneck identification (which requirement is the binding constraint on the whole sequence?)
- Scenario analysis (if the health check is delayed by 2 weeks, what does the critical path look like?)
- Red flag logic improvement (if any node in the critical path has already missed its deadline, the whole case is infeasible — not just that specific requirement)

**Confidence Scoring via RLVR (Weeks 6, 7)**

Patil's RLVR framework (Week 6) and Srivastava's observability-as-feedback-loop argument (Week 7) together suggest a concrete implementation path for confidence scoring in ReloPass's requirements:

1. Define the verification task: given a requirement (lead time, responsible party, document list) and a source URL, does the source confirm this requirement? This is a verifiable binary question.
2. Build an extraction model that reads regulatory source documents and outputs structured requirements.
3. Use RLVR to train this model: reward when extracted requirement matches the authoritative source; penalize when it diverges.
4. Each requirement in the knowledge graph carries a confidence score (source match quality × source freshness × contradiction-check result × lawyer-verified status).
5. Confidence scores drive Case Command's amber/red flag logic: high-confidence requirements display as definitive; low-confidence requirements display as "verify before acting."

#### 2. The Pre-Verification Pipeline as Competitive Moat

**From Manual Research to Automated Extraction (Weeks 5, 6, 9)**

Lochmiller's manufacturing vertical integration insight (Week 3), Patil's evals-first methodology (Week 6), and the Chai de novo extraction analogy (Week 9) together argue for building an automated requirements extraction pipeline as a core competitive moat.

The pipeline should:
1. Monitor authoritative regulatory sources (government portals, official announcements, UDI guidance PDFs, OFII templates) via scheduled scraping and change detection.
2. Detect changes: compare current source content against the stored version; flag differences.
3. Extract structured requirements from changed documents using an LLM-based extraction model trained specifically on immigration regulatory text.
4. Run contradiction checks: compare extracted requirements against existing knowledge graph entries; flag conflicts.
5. Queue for lawyer verification: changes that exceed a confidence threshold are marked as "pending verification" and routed to the designated lawyer for sign-off.
6. Update the knowledge graph after lawyer confirmation.

This pipeline converts the bottleneck (lawyer research time) into a review function rather than a creation function — the same lever Chai applies by making chemists the verifiers of AI-generated antibody candidates rather than the creators of new candidates from scratch.

**Multi-Source Verification Architecture (Weeks 2, 7)**

Srivastava's multi-cloud resilience principle (Week 7) and the broader course theme of verification-over-single-source both argue for requiring multiple independent sources to confirm any requirement before it is hardcoded. The pre-verification pipeline's "contradiction check" phase should:
- Query at least two independent authoritative sources per requirement
- Flag disagreements for manual resolution rather than defaulting to one source
- Track source provenance and last-verified date for every requirement field
- Deprecate requirements whose sources are no longer accessible or authoritative

#### 3. Staleness Detection as a Self-Improving System

**RLVR as the Foundation for Automated Freshness (Weeks 5, 6)**

The most technically specific AI application for ReloPass is using RLVR-trained models for automated staleness detection. The implementation:

1. For each requirement in the knowledge graph, store the exact source URL and the specific text segment that justifies the requirement (the "evidence snippet").
2. Run a periodic freshness check: fetch the current source URL and compare against the stored evidence snippet.
3. Use an LLM to classify the comparison: "unchanged," "updated — same requirement," "updated — different requirement," "source unavailable."
4. Changes classified as "updated — different requirement" trigger the pre-verification pipeline for re-extraction and lawyer review.
5. Changes classified as "updated — same requirement" update the last-verified date without triggering lawyer review.
6. Model performance is evaluated against historical lawyer-confirmed changes: when the model classified something as "unchanged" that a lawyer subsequently corrected, that is a false negative. Use these cases to improve the model.

This is RLVR applied to compliance monitoring: the "verifiable reward" is whether the model's staleness classification matches the subsequent lawyer determination.

**The Feedback Loop from Case Outcomes (Weeks 7, 8)**

Srivastava's observability-as-feedback (Week 7) and Rauch's corporate agent data access argument (Week 8) together suggest that case outcome tracking is a critical, underexploited data source for ReloPass's self-improvement. Every case that goes through Case Command is a labeling event:
- Did the HR generalist follow the Case Command sequence?
- Were any requirements marked as incorrect or outdated after the case completed?
- Did any amber flags materialize into actual delays?
- Were any responsible-party assignments incorrect?

Systematic capture of this data enables:
- Requirement accuracy improvement (cases where the HR generalist flagged an incorrect requirement update the knowledge graph)
- Lead time calibration (cases where actual processing times differed from estimated lead times update the time distribution for that requirement)
- Responsible-party accuracy improvement (cases where the designated party couldn't complete their action may indicate a misattribution)
- Corridor-level risk scoring (corridors with higher rates of amber/red flags or case failures need more frequent freshness checks)

#### 6. The Self-Improving Agent Vision for ReloPass

Drawing on the course's recurring theme of self-improving AI systems, here is a concrete architecture for ReloPass's long-term "self-improving agent" approach to corridor knowledge maintenance:

**The Four-Layer Feedback Architecture:**

*Layer 1 — Source Monitoring (continuous):* Automated scrapers monitor authoritative government sources for each corridor. Changes trigger the extraction pipeline.

*Layer 2 — Extraction and Verification (automated + lawyer-review gate):* LLM-based extraction produces draft updates to affected requirements. Automated contradiction checking compares against existing knowledge graph entries. High-confidence changes are queued for lawyer verification; low-confidence changes are flagged for immediate lawyer review.

*Layer 3 — Case Outcome Feedback (event-driven):* Case outcomes (timeline adherence, requirement corrections, responsible-party changes) feed back into requirement accuracy metrics. Requirements with high error rates are prioritized for source re-verification.

*Layer 4 — Model Improvement (periodic):* Performance data from Layers 1-3 (false negatives in staleness detection, extraction errors flagged by lawyers, case outcome discrepancies) trains the next version of the extraction and staleness detection models via RLVR. This closes the self-improvement loop.

This architecture operationalizes the course's most important concept for ReloPass: the corridor knowledge graph is not a static database to be maintained manually — it is a self-improving system that uses every source change, every lawyer correction, and every case outcome as training signal for the next iteration.

The result, at maturity: a corridor knowledge graph that approaches real-time freshness, with accuracy that improves automatically from the feedback loop, and confidence scores that allow ReloPass's deterministic rule engine to distinguish between "this requirement is verified-current" and "this requirement is due for re-verification." The HR generalist never needs to know how this works — they just see the amber flag when a requirement's confidence score drops below threshold, and the full green-light output when the corridor is fully verified. That is the compliance infrastructure promise: reliable, always-fresh, deterministically correct, with AI doing the continuous maintenance work invisible to the user.
