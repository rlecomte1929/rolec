# Relocation Compliance Checklist Europe: The Complete HR Guide (2026)

Most relocation compliance failures don't happen because HR teams don't care. They happen because the compliance requirements are spread across five different government systems, two countries, and a timeline that runs for months — and no one has a single place to track all of it.

This checklist covers the full lifecycle of a European relocation: from pre-approval through to repatriation. It's structured by phase, not by country, because the obligations follow the employee — not the destination state's paperwork order.

---

## How to Use This Checklist

Work through each phase in order. Some items are sequential (you can't file a residence permit before the work permit is approved). Others can run in parallel (social security registration and tax registration are independent in most countries).

Mark items complete only when you have documentary evidence — not when you've submitted something, but when you've received confirmation.

---

## Phase 1: Pre-Approval (Before Offer Letter)

These checks happen before you extend a formal offer. Skipping them is the most expensive mistake in international mobility — it's far harder to walk back an offer than to do the eligibility check upfront.

- [ ] **Confirm the role qualifies for the target permit category** — EU Blue Card, Skilled Worker, Intra-Company Transfer (ICT), or national category. Each has different salary thresholds, qualification requirements, and processing tracks.
- [ ] **Check if the employee's nationality requires a visa** — EU/EEA nationals have free movement rights. Non-EU nationals require a work permit and (usually) a visa. Check the destination country's visa requirement list by nationality.
- [ ] **Verify qualification recognition** — For regulated professions (engineers, doctors, architects, teachers), check the destination country's recognition database before starting. Germany: ANABIN database. France: ENIC-NARIC. Spain: Ministry of Education equivalency.
- [ ] **Confirm the salary meets the permit threshold** — Most EU work permit categories have minimum salary requirements that change annually. Verify the current threshold for the specific permit type and country.
- [ ] **Check for Posted Worker Directive obligations** — If the employee is remaining on home-country employment contract, Posted Worker rules may apply, requiring registration in the destination country within the first month.
- [ ] **Review social security treaty status** — If the employee is on home-country payroll, an A1 certificate (or equivalent) may be needed to confirm which country's social security system applies.

---

## Phase 2: Pre-Departure (After Offer Acceptance, Before Arrival)

This phase runs 6–16 weeks depending on destination country and nationality. It's the most document-intensive phase.

**Work Permit**
- [ ] File work permit application with the relevant authority (employer obligation in most EU countries)
- [ ] Obtain labour market needs test clearance if required (Spain, some German categories)
- [ ] Receive permit approval or visa authorisation

**Visa (if required)**
- [ ] Book embassy appointment — book the day you file the permit, not after approval
- [ ] Prepare visa application pack: passport, permit approval, employment contract, proof of accommodation, proof of health insurance
- [ ] Collect visa stamp

**Pre-Departure Documents**
- [ ] Original employment contract in destination country's official language (or certified translation)
- [ ] Degree certificates with certified translation if required
- [ ] Criminal record check if required by destination country (Germany, Netherlands, Belgium for some categories)
- [ ] Health insurance confirmation covering destination country from day of arrival
- [ ] Proof of accommodation (rental contract or employer-provided accommodation letter)

**A1 Certificate / Social Security**
- [ ] Request A1 certificate from home-country social security authority if employee stays on home-country payroll
- [ ] Confirm payroll setup in destination country if switching to local payroll

## Phase 3: Arrival (First 30 Days)

The first month is administratively dense. Missing registration deadlines creates compliance gaps that are difficult to remediate later.

- [ ] **Register at local municipality** — Required in Germany (Anmeldung), Netherlands (BRP registration), Belgium (commune registration), Spain (Padrón municipal) within 7–14 days of arrival. Generates the registration certificate needed for almost everything else.
- [ ] **Apply for residence permit / ID card** — In most EU countries, the work permit and residence permit are separate documents. The residence permit application often requires the municipality registration certificate.
- [ ] **Register with social security authority** — If on local payroll, register with the destination country's social security system. Obtain the social security number.
- [ ] **Open a local bank account** — Required for local payroll. Some countries (Germany, Netherlands) allow salary payment to foreign accounts temporarily; check the rules.
- [ ] **Register with local tax authority** — Obtain a tax identification number. Required before first local payroll run.
- [ ] **Confirm health insurance coverage is active** — Public or private, depending on country and payroll status.
- [ ] **Employer posts worker registration** (if Posted Worker Directive applies) — Must be filed in the destination country before or within the first day of work. Late filing carries significant fines in France, Germany, and Belgium.

---

## Phase 4: During Assignment

Ongoing compliance obligations that HR teams most often overlook because they feel like "set and forget" items — until they aren't.

**Permit Renewal**
- [ ] **Track permit expiry date** — Set a reminder 3 months before expiry. Most EU permits must be renewed before they expire; the employee cannot continue working on an expired permit even if renewal is pending.
- [ ] **File renewal application 3 months before expiry** — Some countries (Germany, Netherlands) allow continued work during the renewal period if filed on time. Others do not.

**Tax Compliance**
- [ ] **Confirm tax residency status** — After 183 days in most EU countries, the employee becomes a tax resident. This changes withholding obligations and may trigger double-taxation treaty considerations.
- [ ] **File annual tax return** — Required in the destination country once tax residency is established. Deadlines vary (Germany: July 31; France: May/June; Netherlands: May 1).
- [ ] **Check for shadow payroll requirements** — If the employee remains on home-country payroll but is a tax resident abroad, a shadow payroll may be required in the destination country.

**Immigration Compliance**
- [ ] **Monitor travel patterns** — Extended absences from the destination country can affect permit validity in some jurisdictions.
- [ ] **Update permit if role or employer changes** — A promotion that changes job title or salary, or a company restructuring that changes the employing entity, may require a permit amendment.
- [ ] **A1 certificate renewal** — A1 certificates are typically issued for 12–24 months. Renew before expiry if the employee remains on home-country social security.

---

## Phase 5: Repatriation

The end of an assignment has its own compliance obligations. These are the items most frequently skipped — and the ones that create the longest-tail liability.

- [ ] **Deregister from municipality** — Required in Germany, Netherlands, Belgium, Spain. Failure to deregister creates continued tax and social security obligations in some jurisdictions.
- [ ] **File final tax return in destination country** — A departure tax return is required in most EU countries. Covers income earned through the date of departure.
- [ ] **Surrender or cancel residence permit** — Some countries require physical surrender of the residence permit card; others simply require notification.
- [ ] **Cancel local social security registration** — Notify the destination country's social security authority of the end of employment.
- [ ] **Close local bank account** — Coordinate with payroll team to ensure final salary payment clears before closure.
- [ ] **Cancel local health insurance** if on a private plan — Ensure home-country coverage is reinstated from the date of return.
- [ ] **Retain all documentation for 5 years** — Work permits, tax returns, social security records, and payroll records should be retained for the statutory limitation period (5–7 years in most EU jurisdictions).

---

## Compliance Gaps That Generate the Most Fines

Based on common patterns across European jurisdictions, these are the items that most frequently result in penalties:

1. **Posted Worker registration filed late or not at all** — France and Belgium are particularly active on enforcement. Fines start at €4,000 per worker.
2. **Permit allowed to expire before renewal filed** — Creates a period of illegal working, which carries fines for both employer and employee in most jurisdictions.
3. **Municipality deregistration skipped on repatriation** — Can result in continued tax obligations for years after the employee has left.
4. **A1 certificate not obtained before first work day** — Dual social security contributions can be triggered retroactively.
5. **Employment contract not translated / not compliant with local law** — Particularly common in Spain and France, which have specific mandatory clauses.
