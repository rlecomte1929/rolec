# Work Permit Processing Times by Country: EU Edition 2026

Every delayed work permit is a delayed start date — and a delayed start date is a cost. Between lost productivity, extended contractor fees, and the employee limbo of waiting in their home country, permit processing delays are one of the most common and least visible risks in cross-border hiring.

This guide covers realistic processing timelines for the five most common EU destination countries in 2026, plus what you can do at each stage to avoid the delays that are actually preventable.

---

## Why Timelines Vary More Than You Expect

Official government websites publish "expected" processing times. Those figures are almost always best-case. Real-world timelines depend on:

- **Nationality of the employee** — some nationalities require additional security checks
- **Permit category** — EU Blue Card, intra-company transfer, skilled worker permit, and freelancer permits all have different tracks
- **Document completeness** — a single missing document restarts the clock at many embassies
- **Appointment availability** — embassy appointment queues are the most common cause of delays that no one tells you about in advance

With that context, here are country-specific timelines based on real 2026 case data.

---

## Germany: 6–14 Weeks

Germany is the most common destination for skilled worker relocations in Europe, and the process is well-structured — but not fast.

**The process:**
1. Employer submits job offer and salary verification
2. Foreign Authority (Ausländerbehörde) reviews eligibility
3. If applicable, Federal Employment Agency (BA) approval (4–6 weeks alone for non-EU nationals in regulated professions)
4. Employee collects visa at German embassy in home country

**What slows it down:**
- Degree recognition can add 8–12 weeks if qualifications aren't automatically recognised (check the ANABIN database before you start)
- Embassy appointment availability in major cities like Mumbai, Lagos, or Beijing can add 4–8 weeks on top of permit processing

**What you can control:**
- Submit a complete application. Germany's checklist is public — use it exactly.
- Use the accelerated procedure (§81a AufenthG) if your company is registered — it cuts Foreign Authority processing to 3 weeks.
- Book the embassy appointment the same day you submit the permit application, not after approval.

## France: 4–12 Weeks

France operates through a single online portal (ANEF) for most permit categories, which has reduced processing times compared to five years ago — but the variance is high.

**The process:**
1. Employer files via ANEF or submits via DIRECCTE (for intra-company transfers)
2. Prefecture reviews and issues the visa authorisation
3. Employee applies for visa at French consulate
4. Employee activates the residence permit upon arrival in France

**What slows it down:**
- Prefecture backlogs vary significantly by region — Paris prefectures run 3–4 weeks slower than regional ones
- Intra-company transfer (ICT) permits require additional documentation of the employment relationship between entities

**What you can control:**
- The Talent Passport (Passeport Talent) category covers most skilled workers and has a streamlined track — confirm eligibility before defaulting to a standard permit category.
- Register on ANEF before you start. Account setup issues cause more delays than the substantive review.

## Netherlands: 5–10 Weeks

The Netherlands runs a recognised sponsor scheme (IND — Immigratie en Naturalisatiedienst) that, once your company is registered, makes permit processing significantly faster.

**The process:**
1. Employer (as recognised sponsor) submits permit application to IND
2. IND processes in 2–4 weeks (recognised sponsor track)
3. Employee applies for entry visa (MVV) if required
4. IND issues the combined permit (GVVA) or residence permit

**What slows it down:**
- If your company isn't a recognised IND sponsor, timelines jump to 8–10 weeks and you lose the single-window benefit
- MVV exemptions vary by nationality — check before assuming your employee can enter on a tourist visa while waiting

**What you can control:**
- Become a recognised IND sponsor if you hire regularly into the Netherlands. The registration takes 4 weeks once, then accelerates every subsequent case.
- Highly Skilled Migrant (Kennismigrant) is the fastest category — confirm the salary threshold applies (€5,331/month gross in 2026 for employees over 30).

## Spain: 8–16 Weeks

Spain has the longest and most variable timelines in Western Europe. The process involves multiple government bodies and regional variation adds further unpredictability.

**The process:**
1. Employer applies at the Oficina de Extranjería (provincial)
2. State Employment Service (SEPE) issues a labour market needs test (unless the role is on the shortage occupations list)
3. Employee applies for visa at Spanish consulate in home country
4. Employee registers with Social Security upon arrival

**What slows it down:**
- Labour market needs tests add 3–4 weeks for roles not on the shortage list
- Provincial offices process at different speeds — Madrid and Barcelona are the slowest
- The Beckham Law (special tax regime) applications are separate and have their own queue

**What you can control:**
- Check the shortage occupations list (catálogo de ocupaciones de difícil cobertura) — roles on it skip the labour market test
- Spain's Digital Nomad Visa and the EU Blue Card have faster tracks for qualifying employees
- Engage a local immigration attorney for Spain specifically — the paperwork requirements are less standardised than Germany or the Netherlands

---

## Belgium: 6–10 Weeks

Belgium's work permit system is federally split — Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels-Capital each operate their own immigration offices, which creates complexity if your office location changes.

**The process:**
1. Employer applies for Single Permit with the regional authority
2. Immigration Office (federal) reviews residence eligibility in parallel
3. Combined decision issued to employee
4. Employee applies for visa at Belgian embassy if required

**What slows it down:**
- The parallel processing between regional and federal is the main complexity — if either body requests additional documents, the other's timeline pauses
- Single Permit applications in Brussels run slower than Flanders

**What you can control:**
- Confirm which regional authority covers your office location before starting — Brussels-based companies sometimes mistakenly file with Flemish authorities
- The European Blue Card in Belgium has a streamlined track with salary threshold of €45,612/year (2026)

## The Timeline You Should Actually Plan For

| Country | Official estimate | Realistic range | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 4–8 weeks | 6–14 weeks | Embassy appointment queue |
| France | 2–8 weeks | 4–12 weeks | Prefecture backlog |
| Netherlands | 2–4 weeks (sponsor) | 5–10 weeks | Sponsor registration |
| Spain | 1–3 months | 8–16 weeks | Labour market test |
| Belgium | 4–8 weeks | 6–10 weeks | Regional authority split |

Add 2–3 weeks buffer for document collection and translation. Add another 2–4 weeks if the employee's nationality requires additional embassy processing.

---

## What Compliance-Ready Companies Do Differently

The companies that consistently hit the low end of these ranges share three practices:

1. **They start the permit process at offer acceptance, not contract signing.** Waiting for a signed contract costs 1–2 weeks on average.
2. **They maintain a document checklist per corridor, updated annually.** Requirements change. A checklist that worked in 2024 may be missing a field in 2026.
3. **They book embassy appointments in parallel with permit submission.** This is the single highest-impact change for Germany, Spain, and Belgium.
